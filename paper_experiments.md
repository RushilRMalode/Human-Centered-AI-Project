# Original Paper — Experimentation Setup in Detail

**Paper:** Agarwal, Usunier, Lazaric, Nickel. *System-2 Recommenders: Disentangling Utility and Engagement in Recommendation Systems via Temporal Point-Processes.* FAccT 2024. (arXiv:2406.01611)

**Scope of this document.** A walk-through of *exactly* what the authors did, and how, to produce every result in Section 4 of the paper. The aim is reimplementation-level detail: the data-generation pipeline, the parameter values, the training procedure, and the precise quantity plotted in each figure. This is a companion to `paper_summary.md`, which is the high-level summary; here we go down to the procedural level.

---

## 1. The shape of the evaluation

A few framing choices apply to *every* experiment and are easy to miss in the high-level summary.

- **Synthetic-only.** No real-world dataset is touched anywhere in the paper. Every number on every plot comes from data generated by the authors' own model with parameters they chose. The point of the experiments is to confirm *self-consistency* (the MLE recovers the truth) and *qualitative behaviour* (utility-max beats engagement-max in the regimes the theory predicts), not to demonstrate empirical performance on platform data.
- **Single-user perspective.** The model factorises across users, so every experiment is described, run, and plotted from the perspective of a single (synthetic) user. There is no joint multi-user inference, no cold-start question, no leave-one-out across users.
- **Two metric families.** Estimation experiments measure how close the learned parameters are to the true ones (relative L2 error per parameter). Ranking experiments measure the average per-user utility achieved when items are ranked by one scoring rule versus another, where utility is defined by the ground-truth model.
- **Item embeddings are assumed known.** The paper distinguishes between "learning a recommender" (which would normally require learning item embeddings from content or interactions) and "learning the dual-process parameters from arrival data." Section 4 does the second only — the item embeddings $u^1_j$ and $u^2_j$ are given, generated synthetically, and the question is whether the user-side parameters can be recovered. This idealisation is acknowledged in the paper's Limitations section.

## 2. The common setup (used unless an experiment overrides)

Every experiment in Section 4 starts from this base configuration.

### 2.1 Inventory and embedding dimensions

| Symbol | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $m$ | $1000$ | number of items per user |
| $d$ | $10$ | dimension of every embedding ($u^1_j$, $u^2_j$, $v^1_i$, $v^2_i$) |

Every embedding is a unit-norm vector in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$, so all dot products live in $[-1, 1]$.

### 2.2 Link function

The bilinear scores $\langle v^1, u^1 \rangle$ and $\langle v^2, u^2 \rangle$ are passed through a fixed link:

$$
\varphi(x) = \frac{x + 1}{4}, \qquad \varphi : [-1, 1] \to [0, 0.5].
$$

This bounded, monotone, one-to-one map is exactly Assumption 1 in the theory. Mapping into $[0, 0.5]$ keeps both $\alpha^1$ and $\alpha^2$ small enough that the Hawkes process stays stationary ($\alpha^1 + \alpha^2 < 1$).

### 2.3 Hawkes parameters (default)

| Parameter | Default value | Role |
|---|---|---|
| $\mu$ | $0.3$ | base arrival rate |
| $\beta^1$ | $4$ | System-1 decay (fast) |
| $\beta^2$ | $1$ | System-2 decay (slow) |

These defaults satisfy $\beta^2 < \beta^1$ (the identifiability ordering) with a sizeable gap, so the dual structure is present and easy to recover. Individual experiments deviate from this default in exactly one axis at a time.

### 2.4 User-side embeddings

User vectors $v^1$ and $v^2$ are drawn synthetically: $v^1$ uniformly random in $[-1, 1]^d$ and $v^2$ obtained as a random orthogonal rotation of $v^1$. The rotation makes utility and engagement preferences *correlated but distinct* — the regime where the two-system decomposition is meaningful (if they were identical the problem trivialises; if they were independent the data wouldn't reflect a single coherent user).

### 2.5 Item-side embeddings (the QR-factorisation trick)

The construction of $u^1, u^2$ matters because Experiments 1 and 2 measure error against these embeddings, and Experiments 3 and 4 vary their geometry.

The base recipe (used in Experiments 1 and 2):

1. Draw a random $d \times d$ matrix $A$ with iid entries.
2. QR-decompose: $A = Q R$, so $Q$ is a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix.
3. Take $u^1 = Q_1$ and $u^2 = Q_2$ — the first two rows of $Q$. By construction these are orthogonal unit vectors, so $\langle u^1, u^2 \rangle = 0$.
4. Generate the remaining $m - 2$ item embeddings by taking other rows of $Q$ (or rows perturbed with $\mathcal{N}(0, 1/(10 d))$ noise) and renormalising to unit length.

The result is an item catalogue in which the System-1 axis $u^1$ and the System-2 axis $u^2$ are exactly orthogonal — the cleanest possible disentanglement scenario.

Experiments 3 and 4 modify this recipe; see those sections.

### 2.6 Session structure

A "session" is one realisation of the user actually opening the platform. Sessions arrive over time according to the Hawkes process; within each session the user views a small batch of items.

- **Session-arrival times.** Drawn from the dual-kernel Hawkes process via Ogata's thinning algorithm (Ogata, 1981). The thinning bound is the maximum intensity over the simulation window; candidate times are proposed at that rate and accepted with probability $\lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}$. This produces ground-truth event times.
- **Items per session.** A geometric random variable with success probability $p = 0.8$, clipped to the integer range $[1, 6]$. So most sessions contain 1–2 items, occasionally up to 6.
- **Item identity within a session.** Each item is drawn uniformly at random from the inventory of $m = 1000$. This ensures Assumption 3 (every item is well-covered across sessions).
- **Session summary.** For computing the bilinear $\alpha^j_{ij}$ score that drives the next session, each session is summarised by the *mean* of its constituent item embeddings. So the "per-event mark" used by the model is a $d$-dimensional vector — the average $u^j$ over the items viewed in that session.

### 2.7 Sample / epoch convention

A *sample* (or *epoch*) is a fixed-size chunk of consecutive sessions used as a unit of training data. The paper uses 1024 sessions per sample (the text occasionally rounds to "1000 sessions"). Experiments 1 and 2 sweep the *number of samples* used during training while keeping the per-sample size fixed.

### 2.8 Training

| Choice | Setting |
|---|---|
| objective | maximum log-likelihood of the unmarked Hawkes process (timing only) |
| optimiser | Adam |
| learning rate | $0.002$ (uniform across all parameters) |
| batch size | $16$ samples |
| identifiability anchor | hard clamping: $\beta^1 \ge 1$ and $\beta^2 \le 1$ during gradient updates |

Two things to note about the objective. First, the authors compute the timing-only likelihood — they do *not* include a mark likelihood term, because they are not trying to learn a parametric mark distribution. The marks are used only as session summaries that feed into the bilinear scoring inside $\alpha^j$. Second, the clamping $\beta^1 \ge 1 \ge \beta^2$ removes the label-swap symmetry by force; without it, gradient descent could converge to either of the two equivalent labellings.

What gets jointly learned: $\mu$, $\alpha^1$, $\alpha^2$ (via the bilinear forms, so really $u^1, u^2$ on a per-user basis), $\beta^1$, $\beta^2$, and the user embeddings $v^1, v^2$. Item embeddings $u^1_j, u^2_j$ are held at their generated ground-truth values throughout — that is the "item embeddings assumed known" idealisation.

## 3. Experiment 1 — Sample size vs estimation error (§4.1, Figure 1)

**Question.** Does the joint MLE for the dual-Hawkes recommender model converge to the true parameters as the amount of data grows? In other words, is the consistency promised by Theorem 2 visible at finite sample sizes that one might encounter in practice?

**What is varied.** The number of training samples (recall: each sample contains 1024 sessions). The sweep runs roughly from a few tens of samples up to about 1000.

**What is held fixed.** All of the common setup from §2: $m = 1000$ items, $d = 10$, $\mu = 0.3$, $\beta^1 = 4$, $\beta^2 = 1$, the QR-factorisation item-embedding recipe with $u^1, u^2$ orthogonal, and the Adam settings.

**What is measured.** For every parameter or parameter family, the *relative* L2 error between the learned value and the ground truth:

$$
\text{Err}(\hat\theta) = \frac{\| \hat\theta - \theta^\star \|_2}{\| \theta^\star \|_2}.
$$

For scalar parameters this reduces to a percentage error in absolute value. The plot in Figure 1 (left panel) overlays curves for: $\mu$, $\alpha^1$, $\alpha^2$, $\beta^1$, $\beta^2$, $u^1$, $u^2$.

**What the result looks like.** Every curve declines monotonically with the number of samples. By the time the sample budget reaches $\sim 1000$ (i.e. about a million sessions), every parameter family is recovered to a few percent. The two embeddings ($u^1$ and $u^2$) take the longest to converge but still get there. The headline takeaway: at this scale, the dual-Hawkes parameters are jointly recoverable with no fundamental obstruction, which is exactly what Theorem 2 promises in the asymptotic limit.

## 4. Experiment 2 — Effect of the $\beta^1 - \beta^2$ gap (§4.2, Figure 2 top)

**Question.** Identifiability requires $\beta^1 \ne \beta^2$. How sharply does estimation degrade as the two decay rates approach each other? At what gap does the method effectively stop disentangling the two systems?

**What is varied.** $\beta^1$ is swept across the range $[1, 5]$ while $\beta^2$ is held fixed at $1$. The gap $\beta^1 - \beta^2$ therefore ranges from $0$ (the boundary case, where the two kernels coincide) up to $4$.

**What is held fixed.** Sample budget is 1024 samples (so the sample-size axis from Experiment 1 is held at its high end, isolating the gap effect). Item embeddings are generated by the same QR factorisation recipe as Experiment 1, keeping $u^1 \perp u^2$. Everything else is the §2 default.

**What is measured.** Same relative-L2 error as Experiment 1, but plotted now as a function of $\beta^1 - \beta^2$ instead of sample count. The figure has two panels showing this curve for the timing parameters and the embeddings respectively.

**What the result looks like.** The curves drop monotonically as the gap widens.

- At gap $= 0$ ($\beta^1 = \beta^2 = 1$), the timing parameters $\mu, \beta^1, \beta^2$ are still estimated reasonably well, but the embeddings $u^1$ and $u^2$ have *very* high error: the data simply does not contain enough information to separate the two kernels' contributions, so the gradient is left to fight over identifying the bilinear forms with no anchor.
- As the gap grows, error on the embeddings drops sharply.
- By gap $\approx 4$ (i.e. $\beta^1 \approx 5$, $\beta^2 = 1$), error on every parameter family has converged to its floor.

This is the headline figure for the paper's main caveat: the dual decomposition is identifiable in principle (Theorem 1) but in finite samples the two decay rates have to be *measurably* different for the inference to actually work. This is also the regime our project's marked extension attacks: by adding identifiability through engagement marks, we want to recover the embeddings even when this gap collapses.

## 5. Experiment 3 — Utility vs engagement at varying $u^1$–$u^2$ similarity (§4.3, Figure 2 bottom-left)

**Question.** Does ranking by the (estimated) System-2 score $\langle v^2, u^2 \rangle$ actually achieve higher *utility* than ranking by the engagement signal? And how does that depend on how similar a user's two preference axes are?

**What is varied.** The cosine similarity $s = \langle u^1, u^2 \rangle$ between the System-1 and System-2 axes of the item embeddings. Here, "$u^1$" and "$u^2$" are constructed so that their inner product is exactly $s$; $s$ is swept across negative values (the two axes anti-aligned), zero (orthogonal — the Experiment 1 setting), and positive values (the two axes overlap heavily).

**Item-embedding construction at controlled similarity.** Instead of taking two orthogonal rows of $Q$, the construction here is

$$
u^1 = s \cdot u^2 + \sqrt{1 - s^2} \cdot w,
$$

where $w$ is a unit vector orthogonal to $u^2$. Then $\langle u^1, u^2 \rangle = s$ by construction, and both vectors remain unit-norm. The item-side $u^2$ is taken from the QR factorisation as before.

**The two ranking rules being compared.**

- **Engagement maximisation** ranks items by $\langle v^1, u^1 \rangle + \langle v^2, u^2 \rangle$ — the combined arrival-driving signal, which conflates impulsive and deliberate components.
- **Utility maximisation** ranks items by $\langle v^2, u^2 \rangle$ alone — System-2 only, the "what the user actually values" signal.

**What is measured.** For each user, average ground-truth utility over the top-100 items returned by each ranking rule. Utility is defined as the System-2 score under the *true* parameters (so this is an evaluation against the oracle, not a held-out empirical quantity).

**What the result looks like.** Two curves plotted against dissimilarity ($1 - s$, on the x-axis):

- The utility-max curve (red) is roughly *flat at a high value* — independent of $s$, because it is using the right signal.
- The engagement-max curve (blue) is high when $s$ is high (the two axes are aligned, so engagement-max happens to track utility) but drops as $s$ decreases. At $s = 0$ (orthogonal axes) it is markedly worse; at $s < 0$ (anti-aligned), it is *substantially* worse.

The plot is the cleanest visual statement of the paper's central practical claim: *when the user's impulsive and deliberate preferences differ, an engagement-maximising recommender systematically misranks items relative to utility.*

## 6. Experiment 4 — Utility vs engagement as the $u^2$-aligned inventory grows (§4.4, Figure 2 bottom-right)

**Question.** How does the utility advantage of System-2 ranking scale with inventory size? Specifically, when the catalogue contains a mix of items aligned with $u^1$ ("trap" or moreish items) and items aligned with $u^2$ ("genuinely useful" items), what happens as the catalogue grows?

**What is varied.** The total inventory size — the number of candidate items the recommender ranks over.

**Item-embedding construction.** Items are partitioned: a fraction is generated with embeddings aligned with $u^2$ (so they look attractive to System-2), and the remaining fraction is generated with embeddings aligned with $u^1$ (so they look attractive to System-1, i.e. moreish). Both groups are perturbed by small Gaussian noise and renormalised to unit length, so the alignment is approximate, not exact. The user-side $v^1, v^2$ and the timing parameters are at their §2 defaults.

**The two ranking rules being compared.** Same as Experiment 3 — engagement-max versus utility-max, ranking the entire (now larger) inventory.

**What is measured.** Average ground-truth utility over the top items returned by each rule, plotted against inventory size.

**What the result looks like.** As inventory grows:

- The engagement-max curve plateaus or even degrades — the larger the catalogue, the more System-1-aligned "trap" items there are sitting at the top of the engagement ranking, crowding out the genuinely useful ones.
- The utility-max curve continues to improve — a larger catalogue means more good items for it to find.

The two curves diverge as inventory grows. Practically: the bigger the catalogue (think every modern feed: TikTok, YouTube Shorts, news aggregators), the larger the gap between what an engagement-maximising recommender will surface and what a System-2 recommender will surface. This is the experiment that converts the paper's theoretical claim into an "and it matters more at scale" punchline.

## 8. Caveats and idealisations baked into the experimental design

These are explicit choices the authors made to keep the experiments tractable and theoretically clean. They are also exactly the surface area where follow-up work (including ours) lives.

1. **Item embeddings $u^1_j, u^2_j$ are given, not learned.** In a real platform you would have to learn or derive these from content features or interaction matrices; the paper's experiments side-step that question entirely.
2. **Single-user.** No joint inference across users, no cold-start, no transfer.
3. **Stationary Hawkes only.** No seasonality, no time-varying base rate $\mu(t)$, no content drift. Real platforms have all three.
4. **Geometric session-length and uniform-random item selection.** These are convenient but not psychologically motivated; the paper acknowledges this is a placeholder.
5. **No mark likelihood is fit.** Marks are used only to summarise sessions for the bilinear $\alpha^j$ — there is no parametric mark distribution being learned. This is the gap the project's marked-Hawkes extension fills.
6. **Identifiability requires $\beta^1 \ne \beta^2$ in the data, *and* the training procedure has to clamp the ordering.** Experiment 2 shows that as the gap shrinks, the embeddings become un-recoverable; below the gap floor, the timing channel alone simply does not carry enough information.
7. **The "utility" being maximised in Experiments 3 and 4 is defined by the same model that generated the data.** So these experiments are measuring how well utility-max ranking under the *fitted* model approximates utility-max ranking under the *true* model. They cannot say anything about whether the two-system decomposition itself matches real human behaviour.

## 9. How this maps onto the project's experiments

The project's four experiments (in `core idea/main.tex` and `latex_report/main.tex`) are deliberately structured to extend or stress-test the original paper's experimental claims:

| Original paper | Project experiment | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| §4.1 sample size sweep | Experiment 1 (consistency) | Same sweep, extended to the *marked* model — shows the marked MLE is also consistent. |
| §4.2 $\beta$-gap sweep | Experiment 2 ($\beta$-gap headline) | Same sweep, but now compares marked and unmarked models. The marked curve stays flat exactly where the unmarked one degrades. |
| (no analogue) | Experiment 3 (sample complexity at moderate gap) | New: tests whether marks help even when timing is identifiable. |
| (no analogue) | Experiment 4 (mark informativeness ablation) | New: directly probes the failure-mode corollary by interpolating mark separation from zero to full. |
| (no analogue) | Experiment 5 (held-out forecasting) | New: train/test split with three baselines (marked Hawkes, unmarked Hawkes + iid marks, Poisson + iid marks); evaluates joint predictive log-likelihood, time-rescaling calibration, and held-out z-accuracy. The original paper does not run any held-out predictive evaluation. |

§§4.3 and 4.4 of the paper (the ranking-utility experiments) have no direct analogue in the project — the project is about *parameter recovery* and *predictive evaluation* of the dual-kernel decomposition, not about downstream ranking under per-item embeddings.
